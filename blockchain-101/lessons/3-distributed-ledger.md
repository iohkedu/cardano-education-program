## Distributed ledger

Blockchain is described as both distributed and decentralized. This lesson explores what ‘distributed’ means, and the next one discusses decentralization in more detail.

### Financial transactions

In traditional banking, transactions are verified and processed by an employee, and for larger amounts, perhaps by a supervisor or branch director. This means there is always an authority deciding what is valid or invalid.

Blockchains, however, have no such central authority — everyone is equal. Each transaction must be verified and accepted by all participants.

A ledger is simply a record book of economic transactions. Now imagine running around trying to get thousands of people to confirm a single transaction you created — even digitally, it would be exhausting and completely impractical.

### Distributed ledger technology

A system is called distributed ledger technology (DLT) when all participants have equal rights (with no central authority that could become a single point of failure) and reach agreement – known as consensus – while being spread across many locations. But there’s a challenge. If everyone has equal power, it’s like a crew of pirates trying to elect a captain – everyone votes for themselves. To avoid chaos, the system employs a consensus algorithm – a method by which all participants agree to follow the same rules.

Blockchain is the most common form of DLT. Because its data is stored as a sequence of linked blocks, its structure is linear. Other DLTs may use different data structures, such as graphs.

In short, every participant keeps their own copy of the ledger and is free to manage it however they like. What matters is that, if they follow the consensus algorithm honestly, their ledgers will tend to converge toward the same chain over time. They might not match exactly at any specific moment but from the first block up to a certain depth, everyone shares the same history. As time passes, that common portion grows longer, and eventually honest participants align on the chain defined by the consensus rules.

### Distributed v decentralized

Participants may be spread across different locations, which makes the system distributed and, actually, geographically decentralized too. However, the difference between distributed and decentralized is that distributed focuses solely on physical locations, whereas decentralization covers all aspects of blockchain, which can be either quantifiable or qualitative. A system can appear decentralized simply because its participants are distributed across many places, and that is one form of decentralization. Still, it is only a tiny part of the entire picture. The next lesson discusses decentralization in more detail.

Let’s look at some examples to illustrate the difference:

* **Distributed**, **decentralized**: blockchain
* **Distributed**, **centralized**: search engines like Google – the data and the computation needed to answer a query are spread across many servers, but all of those servers ultimately operate under one controlling authority
* **Not distributed**, **decentralized**: Linux systems – open source, but each installation runs locally
* **Not distributed**, **centralized**: Windows, macOS – owned and controlled by a single entity and installed on individual machines.

Note: These examples are oversimplified and may contain minor inaccuracies, but they illustrate the idea. Decentralization is often misunderstood or underestimated. For example, even an open-source project can be centralized if its developers restrict new contributors to come in, forming a closed group that controls its direction.
